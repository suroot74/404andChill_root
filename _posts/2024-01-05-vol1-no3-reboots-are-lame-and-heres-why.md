<p>There’s something about reboots that’s always rubbed me the wrong way. Maybe it’s because I’ve been around long enough to see the originals. Or maybe it’s just that rebooting a beloved classic feels a bit like reheating leftovers - convenient, sure, but it’s never quite the same. These days, it seems like every streaming service is digging into their archives and saying, “Hey, remember this?” Yeah, I remember. And honestly, I liked it better the first time.</p>

<p>But this issue isn’t just about reboots. It’s also about the plaid-covered rebellion of grunge, the sugary brilliance of snacks that disappeared too soon, and why the 90s never seem to fade from the pop culture radar. So grab your Walkman and a pair of Doc Martens. Let’s take a trip back to when originality wasn’t optional.</p>

---

<h3 id="Story1">Story 1: Reboots Are Lame, and Here’s Why</h3>
<p>You ever watch a reboot and think, “Who asked for this?” That’s how I felt about <i>Fuller House</i>. Don’t get me wrong, I get the nostalgia. But something about it felt... off. The original had its corny charm, sure, but the reboot was like someone turned the corniness up to 11 and forgot what made the show work in the first place. And don’t even get me started on <i>The Matrix Resurrections</i>. It was so busy reminding us how great the original <s>trilogy</s> was that it forgot to try and be great itself.</p>

<p>Then there’s <i>Saved by the Bell</i>. Now, I loved the original, but it thrived in its time - big hair, neon windbreakers, and a world where “psych” was peak comedy. The reboot tried to bring it into the modern era, but it was like watching someone put a Snapchat filter on a Polaroid. Sure, it’s colorful, but it misses the point.</p>

<p>That’s not to say all reboots are bad. <i>Battlestar Galactica</i> proved that if you put thought and care into it, a reboot can bring something fresh to the table. But most of the time? They’re cash grabs, designed to tap into our memories without offering anything new. And maybe that’s what bothers me the most. Nostalgia is powerful, but it deserves better.</p>

---

<h3 id="Story2">Story 2: Grunge Fashion - How We Weaponized Flannel</h3>
<p>When I think about grunge, I think about flannel shirts, combat boots, and a quiet rebellion that didn’t need to scream to be heard. It wasn’t just a look - it was a mindset. The polished, neon-coated vibe of the 80s didn’t speak to us, so we turned to something real, something messy, something that felt like it was ours.</p>

<p>I remember layering flannel over a band tee and thinking I’d cracked the code of effortless cool. It wasn’t about following trends - it was about rejecting them. You weren’t supposed to look polished. You were supposed to look like you just rolled out of bed, grabbed whatever was on the floor, and walked out the door. And somehow, it worked.</p>

<p>Now grunge is back, but it’s different. Designer flannel? Chic combat boots? Kurt Cobain would’ve laughed and rolled his eyes. But maybe that’s the cycle of fashion - we take what was real, sanitize it for mass appeal, and sell it back as nostalgia. The question is: does it still mean the same thing?</p>

---

<h3 id="Story3">Story 3: Retro Snacks - A Love Letter to Discontinued Treats</h3>
<p>Let’s talk about Dunkaroos. Those tiny cookies and sugary frosting weren’t just a snack - they were a status symbol in the cafeteria. If you had Dunkaroos, you were cool, end of story. And what about 3D Doritos? Sure, they were impractical and kind of weird, but that was the point. They didn’t need to make sense - they just needed to taste good.</p>

<p>There’s something about the snacks of the 90s that sticks with me. Maybe it’s because they felt like they were made just for us. They weren’t trying to be healthy or sophisticated. They were bright, sugary, and unapologetically fun. And when they disappeared, it felt like a little piece of childhood went with them.</p>

<p>Now, some of them are making a comeback - Dunkaroos are back, and every so often, Pepsi resurrects Crystal Pepsi for a limited run. It’s not the same, but it’s close enough to remind me of what we lost. And maybe that’s the best we can hope for - little tastes of nostalgia that remind us how sweet it was.</p>

---

<h3 id="Story4">Story 4: Why the 90s Will Never Die</h3>
<p>You know what’s funny? Every decade has its moment of nostalgia, but the 90s? They just won’t quit. Maybe it’s because the 90s were the last analog decade. CDs felt futuristic, Blockbuster was a Friday night ritual, and AIM away messages were the height of self-expression. We were connected, but not too connected, and there was something magical about that balance.</p>

<p>Pop culture didn’t hurt, either. <i>Friends</i>, <i>The X-Files</i>, <i>Clueless</i>, and grunge music all hit at the perfect time. It felt fresh, raw, and real in a way that’s hard to replicate. Even now, people are wearing Doc Martens and listening to Nirvana like it’s 1995. The 90s weren’t just a decade - they were a vibe.</p>

<p>So maybe the 90s aren’t really gone. They’ve just evolved, slipping into our playlists, wardrobes, and even our Netflix queues. And maybe that’s not such a bad thing. After all, who says a vibe ever has to die?</p>
